Seven ways to save and improve

A café can offer its dish on one Friday next month. The project organiser’s contract has ended. Who picks up the phone?

In Kupiškis, five stops on a food route give visitors a taste of the district. Some dishes are available only on certain days or by advance order. The café’s call is an imagined example from our workshop. It raises a question for any public service that depends on several people keeping a promise between them.

Where could we save or improve?

RedQuadrant’s Seven Ways gives you seven places to look. Start with the last time someone tried to take part. A second phone call might mean the first answer was unclear. A perfectly clear answer won’t help a café that loses money on every extra plate.

  1. Shape demand. What did the caller want to do? Make it easy to offer a dish for one day. Count how often people have to call back to get an answer.
  2. Create economies of flow. Follow a change of date from the kitchen to the visitor. Can the person taking the call get the listing corrected in time?
  3. Reduce waste. If the café sends the same update to three people, agree where to record it once and which duplicate step can stop.
  4. Optimise resources. Count the hours spent checking offers, including work done after closing. Who can release paid time and arrange cover?
  5. Effective organisation. Each venue decides what it can serve. Agree who can amend the shared listing and which decisions need the council.
  6. Optimise procurement. If you buy outside help, include time for a local partner to chair the next meeting and practise handling changes.
  7. Change policy. If a grant pays for a launch but excludes running costs, take the cost of an ordinary week to someone authorised to change the rule or the budget.

Could the partnership keep this going?

Stafford Beer’s Viable System Model (VSM) asks how people organise to keep doing work they value as conditions change. Here’s my questions version, applied to the partners who would maintain the route. Ask the people doing the work how each part happens now. One person or meeting may cover several of them.

S1 Are the right things happening?
What can each venue reliably offer, and is that what residents and visitors want?
S2 Are people working together day to day?
When a dish sells out, how does the next person answering the phone find out?
S3 Are we putting resources where they’re needed?
Who can agree paid hours, cover, or expenses across the partners?
S3* What actually happened, and why?
Pick an ordinary Friday. Compare what a visitor was told with what the café served. Let both explain any difference.
S4 What will we need to adapt to?
Ask what winter trading, changes in supply, or the end of the grant would mean for the offer.
S5 What are we here to do and be?
If attracting visitors and involving village residents call for different uses of the money, who gets a say? Who can settle the choice?

One request for the next meeting

Invite two willing venues to choose one offer on two ordinary dates. Agree who will check it by phone and online, how much paid time they have, and which existing meeting will hear what happened. The venues can then decide whether they want to do it again.

If the purpose is resident influence, try a different test. Ask one resident outside the project what they would change. With their agreement, take the proposal to someone who can decide. Give the resident a date for the answer, return with the reason, and ask whether it addresses what they raised.
