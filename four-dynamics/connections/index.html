Connections and distinctions

The four dynamics bring several bodies of practice into conversation. Their terms illuminate different things, and shouldn’t be collapsed into one theory.

Barry Oshry: robust human systems

Oshry’s Organic Systems Framework is the immediate source for four processes: differentiation and homogenisation of the whole, and individuation and integration of its parts. My practical language is segment, blend, empower, and harmonise. Differentiation and individuation express power; homogenisation and integration express love. Each has a necessary contribution, and each can become destructive when pursued without the others.

Oshry’s work also asks how systemic positions shape experience: tops face complexity and accountability, middles are pulled between others, bottoms face vulnerability, and customers experience neglect. Those conditions can help explain why an apparently helpful intervention is received as another threat or burden. They aren’t personality types, and any person can move between these positions.

Stafford Beer: autonomy and cohesion

Beer’s management cybernetics asks what organisation needs to remain viable. Operational units need enough autonomy to respond to their environments, with coordination and mutual support sufficient to keep a coherent whole. An organisation also needs ways to balance current operations with adaptation to a changing world.

That gives a useful connection to autonomy and integration of the parts, and adaptability and stability of the whole. It doesn’t turn the four dynamics into the Viable System Model. VSM specifies functions, communication channels, relationships with environments, and recursive organisation that this four-part picture doesn’t supply.

This is the connection developed in my presentations. It’s an interpretive crosswalk, rather than proof that the theories are equivalent.

Ivo Velitchkov: essential balances

Velitchkov develops autonomy and cohesion, stability and diversity, and exploration and exploitation as continuing balances in organisation. His account helps resist the urge to choose one side permanently: centralise or decentralise, control or freedom, stability or innovation. The appropriate balance depends on the situation, and has to be maintained at several levels.

Use this to examine the conditions around a team. A lively local experiment can be defeated by organisation-wide measures, approval systems, or resource decisions. A balance that works inside one team can impose costs on another.

Sandra Janoff and Marvin Weisbord: differences as resources

Janoff describes differentiation as learning more about different perspectives, and integration as taking those differences in as resources. With Weisbord, her Future Search practice creates conditions for people with different stakes to understand the wider situation, find common ground, and take responsibility for action.

For facilitation, the consequence is concrete: if a group is smoothing over differences, invite perspectives it hasn’t heard. If differences are pulling it apart, help people discover connections they can act on. Participants need room to find that connection themselves. A facilitator declaring ‘we all agree’ can erase the very information the group needs.

Yvonne M. Agazarian: working through differences

Agazarian’s Systems-Centered theory and practice examine how living human systems develop their capacity to recognise and integrate differences. Functional subgrouping is a particular method for exploring similarities and differences while keeping attention on the group’s work.

Janoff explicitly draws on Agazarian in her differentiation and integration account. It doesn’t make every exercise in separating and recombining groups a Systems-Centered intervention. The institute’s account and demonstrations give a better starting point for that method.

Other useful connections

Boundaries. The StretchCon annex draws connections to boundary-spanning practice: strengthening boundaries for specialist safety and capability, understanding and crossing them for learning, and reframing them for shared work. Ask what the boundary makes possible before deciding to remove it.

Generative relationships. The deck points to Brenda Zimmerman’s STAR work, developed in Generative Relationships STAR. Its attention to differences, attunement, joint action, and reasons to work together offers another way to examine whether relationships can generate something new.

Five core practices. Conversations, clarity, learning, culture shaping, and good and clear intent help turn a diagnosis into everyday work. People need ways to speak about the pattern, authority to act, and a recurring occasion to learn from what follows. Read the five-practices material.

Organisation across boundaries. The arm’s-lengthiness tool asks how separate activities should be, and what relationships their work requires. Use the four dynamics to examine the human experience within whichever arrangement you choose.
