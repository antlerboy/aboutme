Four team dynamics

Segment, blend, empower, and harmonise. Diagnose the patterns in a team or organisation, and choose what to change.

Teams need room to develop distinct capabilities, ways to learn across their boundaries, people who can exercise judgement, and enough common purpose to make their contributions work together. These demands never stay settled. The work changes, people change, and yesterday’s useful arrangement can become tomorrow’s obstruction.

The four dynamics give you a way to notice what’s happening and act on it. Look for what’s missing, what’s being overdone, and what happens as people move between them. A team can be strong in all four. An organisation can be weak in all four, too.

The four dynamics

Whole · differentiation

Segment

Give groups enough separation to develop different skills, knowledge, and capabilities.

Identity: ‘I’m part of this team, profession, or specialism.’

Too little: shallow expertise, blurred responsibilities, everyone chasing the same work.

Too much: silos, territoriality, and other teams becoming the enemy.

Whole · commonality

Blend

Share knowledge and experience across groups. Learn about each other, find commonality, and break bread.

Identity: ‘I know my craft, and I can work with these other people.’

Too little: misunderstanding, poor knowledge flow, and little shared experience.

Too much: everyone in everyone else’s business; endless consultation eroding distinct contributions.

Parts · individuality

Empower

Enable each person to develop and use their particular talents, perspectives, and judgement.

Identity: ‘I can bring something of my own and take responsibility for it.’

Too little: waiting for permission, low initiative, and talent left unused.

Too much: individual goals override commitments to others; every person for themselves.

Parts · concerted action

Harmonise

Connect contributions through shared purpose, mutual support, and agreements about how to work together.

Identity: ‘I’m part of something bigger, and my contribution helps it work.’

Too little: disconnected activity, competing priorities, and little sense of direction.

Too much: groupthink, conformity, and dissent treated as disloyalty.

The arrangement matters: segment ↔ blend, and empower ↔ harmonise, are the most immediate balancing relationships. Vertical and diagonal relationships matter as well. These are dynamics to work with, rather than boxes to put people in.

Balance has a rhythm

‘Balance’ can suggest a quiet midpoint. That would miss the point. A specialist team may need concentrated time apart, then a lively exchange with other teams. A person may need space to try something independently, then help relating it to a shared undertaking. There’s no requirement to spend a quarter of the week in each dynamic.

Look at both weight and rhythm: how much of each dynamic people experience, and when they can move into and out of it. One annual awayday won’t compensate for a year of incompatible targets and punished initiative.

Too much can produce its opposite

An insistence on unity can provoke withdrawal, covert resistance, or open rebellion. A highly unified team can define itself against outsiders and become a fiercely defended silo. Conversely, repeated separation and conflict may lead people to demand rigid central control.

This turning into the opposite is enantiodromia. Watch for rebounds, delayed effects, and the dynamic that gets squeezed out. The label helps you recognise a possibility; it doesn’t give you a law that predicts what every team will do.

Sometimes a small, well-placed change is enough: a protected decision, a shared meal, or a different way to open a meeting. Sometimes those gestures do nothing because incentives, workloads, or authority keep reproducing the problem. The dose and the conditions both matter.
